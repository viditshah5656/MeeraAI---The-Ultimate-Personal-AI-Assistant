## 📐 Mathematical Foundations & Formal Derivations

To understand the academic level expected of incoming students, consider the core mathematical models taught in these programs.

### 1. Nonlinear Systems Control & Dynamic Feedback Linearization

Consider a smooth affine control nonlinear dynamical system defined on a smooth manifold $\mathcal{M} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\dot{x} = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m g_i(x) u_i = f(x) + G(x)u$$

$$y = h(x)$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ represents the state vector, $u \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the control input vector, and $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the output vector. $f, g_1, \dots, g_m$ are smooth vector fields, and $h = [h_1, \dots, h_m]^T$ is a vector of smooth scalar functions.

#### Lie Derivatives and Relative Degree
The Lie derivative of a scalar function $h_i(x)$ along a vector field $f(x)$ is defined as:

$$\mathcal{L}_f h_i(x) = \nabla h_i(x) \cdot f(x) = \frac{\partial h_i(x)}{\partial x} f(x)$$

Higher-order Lie derivatives are defined recursively:

$$\mathcal{L}_f^k h_i(x) = \mathcal{L}_f \left( \mathcal{L}_f^{k-1} h_i(x) \right), \quad \text{with } \mathcal{L}_f^0 h_i(x) = h_i(x)$$

$$\mathcal{L}_g \mathcal{L}_f^k h_i(x) = \frac{\partial \left( \mathcal{L}_f^k h_i(x) \right)}{\partial x} g(x)$$

The system possesses a **vector relative degree** $r = [r_1, r_2, \dots, r_m]^T$ at a point $x_0$ if:
1. $\mathcal{L}_{g_j} \mathcal{L}_f^k h_i(x) = 0$ for all $1 \le j \le m$, $1 \le i \le m$, $k < r_i - 1$, in a neighborhood of $x_0$.
2. The $m \times m$ **Decoupling Matrix** (or Advantage Matrix) $A(x)$ is non-singular at $x_0$:

$$A(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 
\mathcal{L}_{g_1} \mathcal{L}_f^{r_1-1} h_1(x) & \dots & \mathcal{L}_{g_m} \mathcal{L}_f^{r_1-1} h_1(x) \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\mathcal{L}_{g_1} \mathcal{L}_f^{r_m-1} h_m(x) & \dots & \mathcal{L}_{g_m} \mathcal{L}_f^{r_m-1} h_m(x)
\end{bmatrix}$$

#### Exact Feedback Linearization Transformation
When the total relative degree $r_{tot} = \sum_{i=1}^m r_i = n$, the system exhibits no internal zero dynamics. Define the diffeomorphism $\Phi(x) = [\xi_1^1, \dots, \xi_{r_1}^1, \dots, \xi_1^m, \dots, \xi_{r_m}^m]^T$ as:

$$\xi_k^i = \mathcal{L}_f^{k-1} h_i(x), \quad 1 \le i \le m, \, 1 \le k \le r_i$$

Differentiating $\xi_{r_i}^i$ with respect to time yields:

$$\dot{\xi}_{r_i}^i = \mathcal{L}_f^{r_i} h_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^m \mathcal{L}_{g_j} \mathcal{L}_f^{r_i-1} h_i(x) u_j$$

In vector notation across all outputs:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \xi_{r_1}^1 \\ \vdots \\ \xi_{r_m}^m \end{bmatrix}^{\bullet} = b(x) + A(x) u$$

where $b(x) = \left[ \mathcal{L}_f^{r_1} h_1(x), \dots, \mathcal{L}_f^{r_m} h_m(x) \right]^T$.

By choosing the feedback control law:

$$u = A^{-1}(x) \left( v - b(x) \right)$$

where $v \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is a synthetic control vector, the nonlinear system is converted into $m$ decoupled integrator chains:

$$\xi_{r_i}^{(r_i)} = v_i$$

### 2. Modern Optimal Control: Infinite-Horizon Continuous-Time LQR

For a linear time-invariant (LTI) state space system:

$$\dot{x}(t) = A x(t) + B u(t), \quad x(0) = x_0$$

We seek the feedback policy $u(t) = -K x(t)$ that minimizes the infinite-horizon quadratic performance index:

$$J(x_0, u) = \int_{0}^{\infty} \left( x(t)^T Q x(t) + u(t)^T R u(t) \right) dt$$

where $Q = Q^T \succeq 0$ (symmetric positive semi-definite) and $R = R^T \succ 0$ (symmetric positive definite).

#### Derivation via the Continuous-Time Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) Equation
Define the Value Function $V(x(t))$ as the optimal cost-to-go:

$$V(x(t)) = \min_{u_{[t, \infty)}} \int_{t}^{\infty} \left( x(\tau)^T Q x(\tau) + u(\tau)^T R u(\tau) \right) d\tau$$

Applying Bellman's Principle of Optimality over a small interval $\delta t$:

$$V(x(t)) = \min_{u_{[t, t+\delta t]}} \left\{ \int_{t}^{t+\delta t} \left( x^T Q x + u^T R u \right) d\tau + V(x(t + \delta t)) \right\}$$

Taking the Taylor expansion of $V(x(t + \delta t))$ around $x(t)$:

$$V(x(t + \delta t)) = V(x(t)) + \nabla V(x)^T \dot{x}(t) \delta t + \mathcal{O}(\delta t^2)$$

Substituting this back and letting $\delta t \to 0$ yields the continuous-time HJB equation:

$$0 = \min_{u} \left\{ x^T Q x + u^T R u + \nabla V(x)^T (A x + B u) \right\}$$

For the quadratic cost and linear dynamics, we assume a quadratic value function $V(x) = x^T P x$, where $P = P^T \succ 0$. Thus, $\nabla V(x) = 2 P x$. The HJB becomes:

$$0 = \min_{u} \left\{ x^T Q x + u^T R u + 2 x^T P (A x + B u) \right\}$$

To minimize with respect to $u$, set the gradient of the bracketed expression with respect to $u$ to zero:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial u} \left( u^T R u + 2 x^T P B u \right) = 2 R u + 2 B^T P x = 0$$

$$u^*(t) = -R^{-1} B^T P x(t)$$

Substituting $u^*$ back into the HJB equation:

$$0 = x^T Q x + (-R^{-1} B^T P x)^T R (-R^{-1} B^T P x) + 2 x^T P A x + 2 x^T P B (-R^{-1} B^T P x)$$

$$0 = x^T \left( Q + P B R^{-1} B^T P + P A + A^T P - 2 P B R^{-1} B^T P \right) x$$

Since this must hold for all $x(t)$, $P$ must solve the **Continuous Algebraic Riccati Equation (CARE)**:

$$A^T P + P A - P B R^{-1} B^T P + Q = 0$$

The optimal control law gain is then given by:

$$K = R^{-1} B^T P$$

### 3. Rigid Multibody Dynamics & Lie Group Kinematics ($SE(3)$)

Robot manipulators and mobile bases move in the special Euclidean group $SE(3)$, defined as:

$$SE(3) = \left\{ T = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0_{1 \times 3} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4} \;\middle|\; R \in SO(3), \, p \in \mathbb{R}^3 \right\}$$

The corresponding Lie algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3)$ consists of spatial velocities (twists) $S$:

$$\mathfrak{se}(3) = \left\{ \hat{\eta} = \begin{bmatrix} [\omega]_\times & v \\ 0_{1 \times 3} & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4} \;\middle|\; \omega, v \in \mathbb{R}^3 \right\}$$

where $[\omega]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric matrix representation of the vector cross product:

$$[\omega]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\ \omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\ -\omega_y & \omega_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Using the **Product of Exponentials (PoE)** formula, the forward kinematics of an $n$-degree-of-freedom manipulator is written as:

$$T_{bs}(q) = e^{\hat{S}_1 q_1} e^{\hat{S}_2 q_2} \cdots e^{\hat{S}_n q_n} M$$

where $q = [q_1, \dots, q_n]^T$ are joint coordinates, $S_i \in \mathbb{R}^6$ are joint screw axes in the base frame, and $M \in SE(3)$ is the home configuration matrix.

The equation of motion for an $n$-link rigid open-chain manipulator is derived via the Euler-Lagrange equations:

$$\frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}} \right) - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q} = \tau$$

where $\mathcal{L}(q, \dot{q}) = \mathcal{K}(q, \dot{q}) - \mathcal{P}(q)$ is the system Lagrangian. This yields the equations of motion:

$$M(q) \ddot{q} + C(q, \dot{q}) \dot{q} + g(q) = \tau + J^T(q) F_{ext}$$

* $M(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the symmetric, positive-definite joint-space inertia matrix.
* $C(q, \dot{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the Coriolis and centrifugal matrix, satisfying the skew-symmetric property: $\dot{M}(q) - 2C(q, \dot{q})$ is skew-symmetric.
* $g(q) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the gravitational torque vector.
* $J(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times n}$ is the geometric Jacobian map converting joint space velocities to end-effector twists: $V_{b} = J(q) \dot{q}$.

## 💻 Algorithmic Formulations & Reference Implementation

Below is a reference Python implementation using `PyTorch` that demonstrates a differentiable nonlinear model predictive controller (or batch optimal controller) utilizing gradient descent over control sequences for an unicycle mobile robot.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class UnicycleNMPC(nn.Module):
    """
    Differentiable Non-Linear Model Predictive Controller for Unicycle Kinematics
    State x = [px, py, theta]^T
    Control u = [v, omega]^T
    """
    def __init__(self, horizon: int = 20, dt: float = 0.05):
        super(UnicycleNMPC, self).__init__()
        self.H = horizon
        self.dt = dt
        
        # State cost matrix Q and Control cost matrix R
        self.Q = torch.diag(torch.tensor([10.0, 10.0, 1.0]))
        self.R = torch.diag(torch.tensor([0.1, 0.05]))
        
        # Actuation limits
        self.v_max = 1.5   # m/s
        self.omega_max = 2.0 # rad/s

    def forward_kinematics(self, state: torch.Tensor, action: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        Euler Integration of Unicycle Kinematics
        x_{k+1} = x_k + v * cos(theta) * dt
        y_{k+1} = y_k + v * sin(theta) * dt
        theta_{k+1} = theta_k + omega * dt
        """
        px, py, theta = state[..., 0], state[..., 1], state[..., 2]
        v, omega = action[..., 0], action[..., 1]

        px_next = px + v * torch.cos(theta) * self.dt
        py_next = py + v * torch.sin(theta) * self.dt
        theta_next = theta + omega * self.dt

        return torch.stack([px_next, py_next, theta_next], dim=-1)

    def compute_cost(self, state_traj: torch.Tensor, control_seq: torch.Tensor, target_state: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        Evaluates stage costs and terminal state costs over horizon
        """
        cost = torch.tensor(0.0)
        for t in range(self.H):
            state_error = state_traj[t] - target_state
            # Quadratic state error
            cost += torch.matmul(torch.matmul(state_error, self.Q), state_error)
            # Quadratic control penalization
            cost += torch.matmul(torch.matmul(control_seq[t], self.R), control_seq[t])
            
        return cost

    def solve(self, current_state: torch.Tensor, target_state: torch.Tensor, iterations: int = 30) -> torch.Tensor:
        """
        Solves MPC optimization using gradient descent over control sequence
        """
        # Initialize control sequence (H, 2)
        control_seq = torch.zeros(self.H, 2, requires_grad=True)
        optimizer = torch.optim.Adam([control_seq], lr=0.05)

        for _ in range(iterations):
            optimizer.zero_grad()
            
            # Clamp controls to physical boundaries
            bounded_controls = torch.stack([
                torch.clamp(control_seq[:, 0], -self.v_max, self.v_max),
                torch.clamp(control_seq[:, 1], -self.omega_max, self.omega_max)
            ], dim=-1)

            # Rollout trajectories
            states = []
            curr = current_state
            for t in range(self.H):
                curr = self.forward_kinematics(curr, bounded_controls[t])
                states.append(curr)

            state_traj = torch.stack(states, dim=0)
            cost = self.compute_cost(state_traj, bounded_controls, target_state)
            
            # Backpropagate cost to optimize control parameters
            cost.backward()
            optimizer.step()

        # Return optimal sequence first control action
        with torch.no_grad():
            return torch.stack([
                torch.clamp(control_seq[0, 0], -self.v_max, self.v_max),
                torch.clamp(control_seq[0, 1], -self.omega_max, self.omega_max)
            ])

# Demonstration
if __name__ == "__main__":
    nmpc = UnicycleNMPC(horizon=15, dt=0.1)
    x0 = torch.tensor([0.0, 0.0, 0.0]) # Origin
    x_goal = torch.tensor([2.0, 2.0, 1.57]) # Goal: (2,2) with 90 deg orientation
    
    u_optimal = nmpc.solve(x0, x_goal)
    print(f"Optimal Initial Command [v*, omega*]: {u_optimal.numpy()}")
```

## 🛑 Critical Bottlenecks & Theoretical Limits

Despite significant advances in robotics and automation, several foundational theoretical and computational bottlenecks remain active areas of research:

### 1. Real-Time Non-Convex Optimization Constraints
Solving Non-Linear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) for high-dimensional underactuated systems (e.g., humanoid robots with $> 30$ degrees of freedom) requires solving non-convex Trajectory Optimization problems within $1\text{--}10\text{ ms}$ control loops. Real-time iteration (RTI) schemes often struggle with local minima and constraint violations during dynamic maneuvers.

### 2. The Sim-to-Real Domain Shift Gap
While reinforcement learning policies trained in massive parallel simulators (e.g., Isaac Gym) show strong performance in simulation, transferring them to physical hardware remains challenging. Unmodeled dynamics—such as joint backlash, non-linear friction, thermal deformation, and uncalibrated compliance—frequently cause performance degradation or instability on physical systems.

### 3. Formal Verification of Neural Network Controllers
Deep neural networks are increasingly used for direct end-to-end perception and control. However, standard Lyapunov-based stability criteria cannot easily be applied to high-dimensional non-linear neural networks. Proving safety guarantees, forward invariance of control barrier functions (CBFs), and worst-case bounds under adversarial noise remains an open challenge.
